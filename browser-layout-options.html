Simulated annealing

Why a search that is allowed to get worse ends up better.

Annotated with pi

The idea is borrowed from metallurgy. Heat a metal until its atoms move freely, then cool it slowly enough that they settle into a low-energy arrangement rather than freezing into whatever configuration they happened to be in.

The optimiser does the same with a temperature parameter. At high temperature it accepts moves that make the objective worse1; as the temperature falls those moves become rare and the search behaves more and more like hill climbing.

Everything then depends on the cooling schedule.2 Cool too fast and you get the numerical equivalent of a brittle casting — a local minimum you cannot leave. Cool too slowly and you have paid a great deal for a very expensive random walk.

1 Why accept worse moves at all?

A strictly improving search only reaches the bottom of the valley it starts in. Accepting a worse move with probability exp(−Δ/T) lets the chain climb out of a shallow basin while T is large; as T falls the move becomes exponentially unlikely, so the chain keeps the basin it found.

2 What makes a schedule “too fast”?

Faster than logarithmic cooling breaks the convergence guarantee. In practice the test is empirical: if independent runs land in different minima, the schedule is still too fast.
